What resveratrol is

Resveratrol is a polyphenol, one of the compounds plants make to protect themselves. Its "trans" form is the one most often studied. Food sources include grape skins, red wine, peanuts, pistachios, blueberries and cranberries, but the amounts are small, so supplements usually extract it from Japanese knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum).

What the research says, honestly

Resveratrol has been studied for a long time, but the human evidence is mixed. Memorial Sloan Kettering's integrative-medicine database summarizes it this way: human studies are often small or limited; a meta-analysis found no benefit on cardiovascular risk factors; trials in older adults did not show a cognitive benefit; and a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence for use in diabetes. So we do not describe it as protecting the heart, the brain or blood sugar. The seller's own claim is modest: "supports healthy aging." That's a general wellness statement, not a medical one.

Who should ask a doctor first

  • Blood thinners or antiplatelet drugs (for example warfarin): resveratrol inhibits platelet aggregation in lab studies, so combining them may raise bleeding risk.
  • Medicines processed by liver enzymes (CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9, 1A2): resveratrol can change how some drugs are broken down.
  • Hormone-sensitive conditions: resveratrol has estrogen-like activity.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding, under 18: don't use it; the seller's label advises against use while pregnant or nursing, and it is not intended for anyone under 18.
  • Upcoming surgery: tell your surgeon about every supplement you take.

Who it may suit
